  - question: "Which type of loft conversion gives the most space?"
    answer: "A mansard, because it reconstructs the roof to something close to vertical on one side and gains the most floor area and head height of any option. It is also the biggest structural job and the most likely to need planning permission. Below that, a hip to gable combined with a rear dormer gains a great deal on a hipped house — the hip to gable squares the plan off and the dormer adds head height across the back. A rooflight conversion gains no space at all: it uses what the roof already has."
  - question: "Can I have a loft conversion if my roof has trussed rafters?"
    answer: "Yes. Modern trussed roofs are converted all the time. The difference is that the W-shaped webs filling the loft are holding the roof in shape, so new structure has to take that job over before they can come out. That is more work than converting an older cut roof, where the void is already open, and it shows up in the price. What it does not do is rule the conversion out, so do not let anyone tell you a trussed roof cannot be done."
  - question: "Do I need a dormer or will rooflights be enough?"
    answer: "It depends on one measurement, and it is not the one most people take. What matters is the head height left once the new floor structure and the insulated roof build-up have taken their share, not the height you can measure from the ceiling joists to the ridge today. If there is comfortably enough after that, rooflights will give you a room. If it is marginal, a dormer is the honest answer, because a dormer creates head height where rooflights only borrow what is already there."
  - question: "Can you combine two types of loft conversion?"
    answer: "Frequently, and on a hipped house it is the normal thing to do. Hip to gable plus a rear dormer is the standard pairing on a 1930s semi: the hip to gable turns a triangular loft into a rectangular one, and the dormer then adds head height across the back. The one thing to watch is the permitted development volume allowance, because both draw on the same pot. That is why the two get designed together rather than one at a time."
  - question: "Which loft conversion is cheapest?"
    answer: "A rooflight conversion, because the roof is never altered — no structure built out, no new roof covering, no external finishes and usually no planning application. But cheapest only matters if it gives you a room worth having. Paying less for a loft you can still only stand up in down the middle is not a saving. Work out what your roof can actually give you first, then look at cost."
  - question: "Does the type of conversion change whether I need planning permission?"
    answer: "Considerably. Rooflights normally need no application at all. A rear dormer is often permitted development if it stays within the volume allowance and the other conditions. A front-facing dormer usually needs permission, because an extension beyond the roof plane on an elevation fronting a highway is not permitted development. A mansard rarely sits inside the conditions. None of it applies on designated land such as a conservation area. Building Regulations approval is required for all of them, with no exceptions."

Nearly everybody who rings about a loft conversion has already decided what they want. Usually it is a dormer, because that is what the neighbour had.

Sometimes they are right. Often the house wants something else, and occasionally the house cannot have what they have asked for at all. So before the types, three questions about your roof — because the roof decides this, not the brochure.

## 1. How much head height is actually left?

This is the first measurement and the one most people get wrong, because they measure the wrong thing.

What matters is not the height from the existing ceiling joists up to the ridge today. It is what is left after two things have taken their share: the **new floor structure** that has to go in on top of the ceiling joists, because those joists were sized to hold up a plasterboard ceiling and nothing else, and the **insulated roof build-up** that comes down off the rafters. Both are substantial, and both take their thickness out of the middle of the room where the height actually is.

If there is comfortably enough left afterwards, you have options. If there is not, your options narrow to the ones that create head height rather than use it.

## 2. What kind of roof structure have you got?

Put your head through the hatch and look at what is holding the roof up.

**A traditional cut roof** has rafters, purlins and a ridge, with an open void underneath you could walk around in. Older houses generally have these, and they are the easier starting point because the space is already there.

**A trussed roof** has W-shaped webs filling the loft from end to end. Houses from roughly the 1960s onwards usually have these. The webs are not storage obstacles — they are structurally holding the roof in shape, and new structure has to take that job over before any of them come out.

A trussed roof does not stop you converting. It makes it a bigger job, and that shows up in the cost. Anyone who tells you a trussed roof cannot be converted is telling you they would rather not.

## 3. What shape is your roof?

Stand at the end of the house and look at it.

**If the end finishes at a flat vertical wall**, that is a gable. The loft runs full width right to the end.

**If the end slopes inwards**, that is a hip. The loft narrows from both sides as it runs towards the end and then stops, so you lose the end of the loft and a wedge either side of it. That is the single biggest constraint on a lot of semis round here — and it is also the one with a direct fix.

That photograph shows both on one pair of houses. The left-hand half still has its original hip; the right-hand half has been squared off.

**Mid-terrace?** You have gable walls on both sides already. There is no hip to convert, so that option is off the table and the answer will be a dormer or rooflights.

## The four types, and who each one suits

### Rooflight, or Velux conversion

The roof shape does not change. Windows go into the existing slope and all the work happens inside.

**Suits you if** the loft already has the head height. **Rules it out if** it does not, because this is the one option that creates no space whatsoever — it only makes the space you have usable. Quickest, least disruptive, cheapest, and least likely to need planning permission. More on [Velux and rooflight conversions](/services/velux-loft-conversions).

### Dormer

A box built out from the roof slope, replacing part of the slope with a vertical wall and a flat ceiling.

**Suits you if** the head height is marginal, or the loft is usable down the middle but useless either side. A rear dormer is the most common conversion on terraced and semi-detached houses for good reason. **Watch out for** front-facing dormers, which usually need planning permission. More on [dormer loft conversions](/services/dormer-loft-conversions).

### Hip to gable

The sloping hipped end is built up vertically to form a gable wall and the roof extended over it, turning a triangular loft into a rectangular one.

**Suits you if** your roof is hipped — semis, end-terraces, detached houses, bungalows. **Rules it out if** you are mid-terrace, because there is no hip. Usually paired with a rear dormer, because the two solve different problems: the hip to gable gives floor area, the dormer gives head height. More on [hip to gable conversions](/services/hip-to-gable-loft-conversions).

### Mansard

The roof is substantially reconstructed, with one side brought close to vertical. The most space of any option, the biggest structural job, and the most likely to need planning permission.

**Suits you if** the other options will not give you what you need and the budget allows for a largely rebuilt roof.

## Working through it

Roughly in this order:

1. **Is there enough head height after the floor and roof build-ups?** If comfortably yes, rooflights may be all you need. If marginal, you are looking at a dormer.
2. **Is the roof hipped?** If yes, a hip to gable is on the table and will probably be worth pairing with a rear dormer. If you are mid-terrace, it is not.
3. **Cut roof or trusses?** Does not change which type suits you. Does change the amount of structural work and therefore the cost.
4. **What is the room for?** A bedroom with an ensuite needs floor area for a shower under the slope and a sensible run to the soil stack. A home office needs less. This decides how much space you actually have to find.
5. **Where can the stairs land?** Frequently the thing that settles the whole layout, and occasionally the thing that rules a conversion out. The stairs have to come off an existing landing and meet the headroom rules, and a loft that works structurally can still be awkward if there is nowhere sensible to put them.

## What it actually comes down to

The type is a conclusion, not a starting point. Head height, roof shape and where the stairs can land decide it between them, and all three are settled with a tape measure in your loft rather than from a website.

What is worth knowing before anyone comes out is which questions matter, so you can tell whether the person standing in your loft is answering them or just quoting for the thing you asked for.
