faqs:
  - question: "Should you take the skirting boards off to fit laminate flooring?"
    answer: "If you want the best finish, yes. The alternative is leaving the skirtings on and covering the expansion gap with scotia or quadrant beading round the whole room, which reads as an afterthought and gives you another edge to clean. Taking the skirtings off means the flooring runs to the wall with its expansion gap hidden behind the skirting when it goes back on. It is more work, and in a hallway with several doorways it is noticeably more work, but the floor then looks like part of the house rather than something laid on top of it."
  - question: "Why cut underneath the door frame instead of cutting the flooring round it?"
    answer: "Because scribing flooring round a door lining never looks right, particularly with a herringbone pattern where you would be cutting across the angle. Undercutting the lining and the architrave by the thickness of the floor and its underlay lets the boards slide underneath, so the floor disappears under the frame with no visible joint and no trim. The trick is to use an offcut of the actual flooring sitting on the actual underlay as your height gauge, then run the saw flat on top of it."
  - question: "Do you need underlay under laminate flooring?"
    answer: "Yes. Underlay takes up small irregularities in the subfloor, cuts down the hollow sound laminate makes underfoot, and on a solid floor it matters for moisture. Fibreboard is a good choice where you want a bit of levelling as well as the acoustic side, because it is more substantial than a thin foam roll. But underlay is not a substitute for a level subfloor — deal with that first, or every high spot telegraphs through and the joints get worked loose over time."
  - question: "Should laminate flooring joints be glued?"
    answer: "Most click laminate is designed to be laid dry, and normally that is exactly what you do. Gluing the joints with a waterproof PVA is worth considering in a hallway, a kitchen or anywhere that gets mopped, because it stiffens the floor and it stops water finding its way down through the joints from a spill or a wet mop. It is worth knowing the trade-off: a glued floor cannot be lifted and relaid, and some manufacturers have views on it, so check the specification for the floor you are laying."
  - question: "Does gluing the joints make a laminate floor waterproof?"
    answer: "No, and it is worth being clear about that. Gluing the joints means an ordinary spill or a light mop does not get down between the boards, which is where laminate usually fails first. It does not make the board itself waterproof, and it will not save a floor from a burst pipe or a washing machine leak. Think of it as sealing the weak point rather than changing what the material is."

A hallway is the hardest room in the house to floor well and the easiest to floor badly. It is narrow, so everything is on show. It has more doorways per square metre than anywhere else. And it takes every pair of wet boots that comes through the front door.

This one was fitted in grey oak herringbone laminate. Herringbone puts the pattern on display, which means a narrow hallway is unforgiving of anything out of true — so most of the work happened before a single board went down.

## Getting the subfloor level first

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that decides whether the floor is still tight in three years.

Laminate is a floating floor. It is not fixed down, so it sits on whatever is underneath and follows it. A high spot means the boards rock over it every time somebody walks across, and that working back and forth is what eventually opens the joints. Underlay will absorb a little, but it is there to take up small irregularities, not to bridge a hollow.

So the subfloor was checked and levelled before anything else was brought in.

## Fibreboard underlay

  <img src="/images/blog/herringbone-laminate-flooring-hallway/herringbone-laminate-being-laid-on-fibreboard-underlay.webp" alt="Herringbone laminate being laid over green fibreboard underlay in a hallway, with skirtings removed" width="1080" height="1920" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Green fibreboard underlay down across the hallway, with the herringbone going in up the middle.</figcaption>
</figure>

Green fibreboard went down over the levelled subfloor. It is more substantial than a thin foam roll — it takes up the last of the small variation, it takes the hollow, drummy sound out of the floor, and it gives a solid, quiet feel underfoot that you notice most in a hallway where every footstep echoes.

You can see in that photograph that the skirtings are already off and the boards are going in up the centre of the hallway, with the pattern working out towards both walls.

## Skirtings off, door linings undercut

Two jobs here, and between them they are the reason the finished floor has no beading anywhere.

**The skirtings came off.** A floating floor needs an expansion gap round every edge, and you have two options for hiding it: scotia beading round the whole room, or take the skirtings off and put them back down on top of the new floor. Beading is quicker. It also looks like beading. With the skirtings off, the gap sits behind them and the floor simply runs to the wall.

**The door linings were undercut.** Rather than trying to scribe boards round the shape of a lining and architrave — which is difficult at the best of times and worse on a herringbone where the cuts fall across the angle — you cut the bottom off the lining instead and slide the floor underneath.

That photograph shows the whole method. An offcut of the flooring sits on the underlay, and the saw runs flat across the top of it. That sets the cut at exactly the right height — the thickness of the underlay plus the thickness of the board — without measuring anything, and it cannot drift.

  <figcaption>Cut through and cleaned out, ready for the boards to run under.</figcaption>
</figure>

Done properly the floor disappears under the frame. No joint, no trim, nothing to catch a hoover on.

## Gluing the joints

The joints were glued with a waterproof PVA as the floor went down.

Most click laminate is laid dry and that is normally the right thing to do. A hallway is one of the places where it is worth doing differently. Gluing the joints does two things: it stiffens the whole floor into one sheet rather than a lot of loose boards clicked together, and it closes the route water takes when somebody mops or drops something — because the joints are where a laminate floor lets water in and where it starts to swell and lift.

**Being straight about what that does and does not do.** It means a spill or a light mop does not find its way down between the boards. It does not make laminate waterproof — the board is still what it is, and no amount of glue will save it from a burst pipe. It also means the floor is down for good; a glued floor cannot be lifted and relaid.

The things worth looking at in those two are the edges. The floor meets the skirtings with no beading. It goes under the door linings with no visible joint. Nothing has been trimmed over to hide a gap.

That is the difference between a floor that has been laid and a floor that has been fitted, and nearly all of it comes from the preparation rather than the laying.
