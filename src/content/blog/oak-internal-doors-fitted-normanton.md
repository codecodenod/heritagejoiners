faqs:
  - question: "How long does it take to hang an internal door?"
    answer: "A straightforward door into a sound, square lining is an hour to an hour and a half. That is the exception rather than the rule. Most doors need the leaf trimming on at least two edges, the hinge positions marking from the existing knuckles, new hinge recesses cutting, the latch and striker setting out and the door easing until the margin is even all the way round. Two hours a door is a fairer average, and a door going into a twisted lining or an opening that has dropped can take longer than that on its own."
  - question: "Do internal doors have to be trimmed to fit?"
    answer: "Nearly always. Doors are made to standard sizes and openings in real houses are not standard, particularly in older housing stock where the floors have settled. A door is supplied slightly oversize on the basis that it will be cut down. The thing to watch is how much you take off and from where — on most doors there is a limit to how much can come off any one edge before you cut into the lock block or through the lippings, and taking it all off one side throws the stiles out of balance so the door looks wrong even when it shuts properly."
  - question: "Can you hang new doors on the existing frames?"
    answer: "Usually, and it is normally the sensible thing to do. If the lining is sound, square enough and firmly fixed, there is no reason to rip it out — new doors on existing linings is a much smaller job than replacing both. The lining gets checked for plumb and winding first. Where a lining has twisted or is loose it can often be packed and refixed rather than replaced, and only where it is beyond that does it need taking out."
  - question: "What is the gap supposed to be around an internal door?"
    answer: "Around 2 to 3mm down each side and across the top is the normal working margin — enough that the door is not binding when the timber takes up moisture, tight enough that it does not look like a gap. At the bottom it depends on the floor finish: a few millimetres over a hard floor, more where carpet and underlay are going down afterwards, and more again where a door needs to pass over a threshold strip. Getting that bottom clearance right before the flooring arrives saves taking every door off again later."
  - question: "Why do new doors catch or stick after they are fitted?"
    answer: "Two common reasons. The first is moisture: a door that was stored somewhere damp, or hung in a house that is still drying out after plastering, will take up moisture and grow across its width. The second is that the hinges were not set deep enough, so the door is standing proud on the hinge side and binding on the latch side. Both are fixable, but the second one is avoidable, and it comes down to cutting the recesses properly in the first place."

There is not much mystery to this work. It is the sort of job that looks like nothing in the photographs and takes a day and a half, because almost none of the openings in a lived-in house are square and every door has to be fitted to the hole it is actually going into.

## The openings come first, not the doors

Before a door comes out of its wrapper the lining gets checked — plumb on both legs, level across the head, and checked for winding, which is the one people miss. A lining can be dead plumb on each side and still be twisted, so that the two legs are not in the same plane. Hang a flat door in a twisted lining and it will touch the stop at one corner and stand off it at the diagonally opposite one, no matter how well the door itself is fitted.

That is worth finding out at the start. A twisted lining can usually be packed out and refixed, which takes twenty minutes. Discovering it after the door is hung and the hinges are cut means doing the job twice.

## Fitting the leaf

Doors come slightly oversize because they are meant to be cut down. The question is where the material comes off.

Take it all off one side and the two stiles end up visibly different widths, which looks wrong even on a door that shuts perfectly — your eye reads the frame of the door, not the gap. So the width comes off both stiles, split as evenly as the opening allows. The same goes for the rails if the height needs reducing by much.

There is a limit on all of it. On a door with a lock block you can only take so much off the latch stile before you are cutting into the block the latch needs to land in, and on a lipped door you can go through the lipping and expose the core. Knowing where that limit sits on the door in front of you is most of the skill in fitting one.

Margins through the house came out at a couple of millimetres down each side and across the head, with the bottom clearance set for the floor finish that was going down afterwards.

## Hinges, and why doors bind

Most doors that catch do it because the hinges were not let in properly.

A hinge recess has to be cut to the exact thickness of the hinge leaf. Cut it shallow and the leaf stands proud, which holds the door off the lining on the hinge side and pushes it hard against the stop on the latch side. Cut it too deep and the door is pulled in, the hinge is under strain every time it shuts and the screws eventually work loose.

Where new doors go onto existing linings there is a second question, which is whether to reuse the old hinge positions. Usually yes — the recesses are already cut in the lining and matching them means the lining does not need making good. The new door is marked off the lining rather than measured, which is more accurate and quicker than both.

## Making a set of doors look like a set

When a whole house is being done at once there is a point beyond the individual doors, which is whether they read as one set when you walk round.

That means handles at a consistent height, hinges the same way up, and — the one that gets overlooked — the doors picked so the grain is going in a sensible direction and the colour is not jumping between rooms. Oak varies. Doors out of the same pack can be noticeably different, and if you hang them in the order they came off the van you will end up with a pale one next to a dark one on the same landing.

Five minutes standing them up and sorting them before anything is cut solves it for nothing.

The door at the bottom of the stairs is the awkward one in most houses, because the architrave and the newel post are competing for the same few inches of wall. It gets set out so the architrave margin stays consistent with every other door and the difference is taken up where it does not show.
