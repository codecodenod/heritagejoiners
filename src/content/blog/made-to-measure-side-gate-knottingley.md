This was a side passage gate for Tracy, at a house in Brotherton, Knottingley. The passage runs between the gable wall of the house and the neighbouring fence line, and the opening it had to close was 870mm wide.

Which is where most gate jobs go wrong before anyone picks up a saw. You cannot buy a gate for an 870mm opening. You can buy a gate that is roughly that size and then spend an afternoon trying to persuade it to fit, which is a different thing entirely.

So this one was made to the opening.

## The opening sets the size, not the gate

A gate is never the same size as the hole it goes in, and the difference is not a rounding error.

On one side you need room for the hinges to sit and swing without the gate binding on the stile. On the other you need a working clearance so it closes without scraping, because timber moves with the weather and a gate that just fits in October will not in February. At the bottom you need a gap, because the ground is not flat and it will not stay where it is.

Add all of that up and the leaf is meaningfully narrower than the opening. This gate was made 1,550mm high and cut to suit the 870mm opening with those clearances built in — not an 870mm gate squeezed into an 870mm gap.

That is the whole argument for made-to-measure on something like this. An off-the-shelf gate comes in standard widths, and the gap in your wall does not.

## A frame round the outside, boards over the face

There are three ways to build a gate like this, and it is worth being precise about which one this is.

A **plain ledged and braced** gate is boarding held together by horizontal ledges with a diagonal brace between them, and nothing at all round the outside. Perfectly sound, and what you will usually find on a field gate or a shed door.

A **true framed, ledged and braced** gate has a frame of stiles and rails with the boarding **set into a rebate or groove machined in it**. That is the traditional joiner's version. It is the strongest and the best looking, and it is also the most work and the most expensive way to build one.

This gate is the sensible middle option. It has a frame — upright stiles down both edges and a rail across the top — with the **boarding fixed over the face of the frame rather than housed into a rebate**. So it is not a true framed, ledged and braced gate, and it would be wrong to call it one.

What it does give you is most of what the frame is actually for. The hinges screw into a solid stile instead of into the edge of a board. The bolt shoots into a solid stile instead of into the edge of a board. When somebody pulls the gate shut by its edge, as everybody does, that load goes into the frame. You get the framed appearance and the strength where it counts, without the machining a rebated frame needs — which on a domestic side gate is the right balance of cost and job.

## Three ledges and a brace that actually works

Three horizontal ledges across the back, and one diagonal brace.

The brace is the part worth looking at in that first photograph. It runs **up from the bottom hinge side towards the closing side**. That direction is not a style choice — it is the only direction that works.

A gate wants to drop at the far corner, away from the hinges. A brace running up from the bottom hinge puts itself in compression against that, pushing the closing edge up, and timber in compression simply holds. Run the brace the other way and it is in tension, which means the only things resisting the sag are the joints and the fixings. They will lose eventually, and you end up with a gate that catches on the floor and gets lifted every time it is opened.

Get the brace the wrong way round and everything else you did well stops mattering.

## Timber: treated softwood, 4×1 and 4×2

The gate is treated softwood throughout, from 3.6m lengths, in two sections: **4×2 for the frame and ledges**, **4×1 for the boarding**.

That split is deliberate. The frame and ledges are doing the structural work and want the thickness. The boarding is closing the gap and keeping eyes out, and heavier boards there would only add weight for the hinges to carry without making the gate any stronger.

Pressure-treated timber is the sensible material for a gate that lives outside and will not be looked after. It resists rot without needing a maintenance routine nobody will stick to, and it is a fraction of the cost of a hardwood that would want oiling.

Two **350mm black T-hinges**, and a **150mm black padlockable bolt**.

The hinges are long for the size of gate on purpose. A T-hinge spreads its load along the ledge rather than concentrating it at the edge, and the longer the strap the further that load travels into the gate. On a gate that gets pushed, pulled and occasionally leaned on, that is where the money goes.

The bolt is padlockable, which gives the choice without forcing it. Day to day the gate sits on the bolt. If you want it locked, the hasp takes a padlock. The padlock itself was not supplied — people have their own views on size and type, and it is a two-minute job to add one.

## Hung off the house wall

The other half of the job is what the gate hangs on, and here it is the building itself.

One side is the gable wall of the house, with a timber hanging stile fixed securely back to the brickwork. The other side closes against the existing concrete fence post that was already there.

On a narrow passage that is usually the better answer than digging a post in. There is often nowhere to put one, the ground is frequently concrete or flags, and a post concreted into a narrow gap is never as solid as a wall that has been standing for decades. What matters is that the stile and its fixings are sound, because every bit of weight in that gate is going through them.

Working to what is already there — a wall on one side, somebody else's post on the other, neither of them plumb or square to each other — is the ordinary reality of this kind of job. It is also exactly why the gate had to be made rather than bought.

## What was not included

Worth being straight about the scope.

The gate is treated timber and that is the finish. No stain or paint was applied — the treatment is what protects it, and a decorative coating is best left until the timber has dried out and settled, if it is wanted at all. No padlock was supplied with the bolt.

The species of the softwood was not recorded, so it is not claimed here. It is treated softwood, which is what the job wanted.
