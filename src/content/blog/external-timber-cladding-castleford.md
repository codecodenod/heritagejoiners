External timber cladding on a garage wall in Castleford. The job was to cover the exposed side of a garage with vertical timber boarding, giving the wall a cleaner appearance and a finish that worked with the garden around it.

Rather than fixing boards straight onto the existing wall, we built a treated timber framework first. That gave secure fixing points and let the boarding be set out properly.

## External timber cladding for a garage wall

The wall was a plain garage elevation facing straight into the garden. Nothing wrong with it structurally — it just did nothing for the space it was sitting in.

Cladding a wall like this is not complicated work, but it is easy to get wrong. Boards that wander off line, a framework that doesn't hold them flat, or fixings that rust and streak the timber will all show for as long as the cladding is up.

## Preparing and battening the garage wall

The wall was battened using 2 × 1 inch pressure-treated roofing battens.

Setting the battens out correctly is the part that decides whether the finished job looks right. They had to give consistent support across the full width of the wall while working around the existing tree, the adjoining walls and the changes in height along the elevation.

The framework also holds the new boarding off the original surface rather than trapping it tight against the garage wall.

## Fitting the vertical timber cladding

Pressure-treated rough-sawn boards were then measured, cut and fitted vertically across the wall.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/timber-cladding/first-vertical-timber-cladding-boards-plumb.webp" alt="First vertical timber cladding boards fixed to the battens and checked plumb" width="1080" height="1920" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>The first boards on, checked plumb against the batten framework.</figcaption>

The first board sets the line for everything after it, so it gets checked plumb before anything else goes up. Any lean in it carries across the whole wall and shows worse with every board you add.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/timber-cladding/vertical-timber-cladding-installation.webp" alt="Vertical rough-sawn treated timber boards being screwed to the battens" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Boards going on one at a time, each one checked against the last.</figcaption>
</figure>

Every board was positioned individually to keep the line consistent across the finished run.

Vertical boarding suited the proportions of this wall. It draws the eye up rather than along, which is what turns a flat garage elevation into something that reads as a deliberate timber feature.

## Why the boards were screwed rather than nailed

The boards were face-fixed with corrosion-resistant exterior-grade screws suitable for treated timber.

Screwing gives better control when pulling each board into position, and a more secure mechanical fixing into the battens behind. It also means a single board can be taken off and replaced later without dismantling a whole section of cladding.

All external timber fixings need proper corrosion protection — stainless steel, hot-dip galvanised, or a suitable high-performance coated steel. Treated timber will eat cheap fixings, and once they start to go you get rust streaks running down the face of the boards.

## Almost completed garage wall cladding in Castleford

The wall was finished with treated timber detailing along the top and base, which gives a defined edge and protects the exposed ends of the boarding.

The finished cladding changed the wall completely. The rough-sawn timber gives a natural, straightforward finish, and the vertical lines make the area feel cleaner and more enclosed than the bare wall did.

It is practical outdoor carpentry rather than fine joinery, but it still comes down to setting out, accurate cutting, and dealing properly with the awkward details around what is already there.
