  - question: "What are purlin struts and why are they in the way?"
    answer: "Purlins are the big horizontal timbers running along the roof slope, carrying the rafters between the walls. Because a purlin spans a long way, it usually needs propping somewhere along its length, and the props are the struts — angled timbers running down from the purlin to a load-bearing wall or a binder below. They do a necessary job, and they land in the worst possible place, which is the middle of the loft floor at about head height. That is the thing that makes most unconverted lofts feel unusable even when the head height is fine."
  - question: "Can you remove the struts in a loft?"
    answer: "Not on their own, no. The struts are holding the purlins up and the purlins are holding the roof up, so taking them out without replacing what they do is how roofs sag. What you can do is give the purlin a different way of being supported, so the strut is no longer needed — which is what happened here. That is structural work: it needs designing and calculating properly and it is inspected by Building Control. It is not something to take a saw to on a Saturday."
  - question: "What is the difference between a loft room and a loft conversion?"
    answer: "Mostly the staircase, and what that means legally. A loft conversion creates a habitable room under the Building Regulations, which requires a fixed staircase, a protected escape route, fire doors to the rooms off it and a completion certificate at the end. A loft room is a boarded, plastered, usable space reached by a ladder. It can be a perfectly good room — this one is — but it is not a habitable room in the Building Regulations sense, and it should not be described or sold as a bedroom."
  - question: "Does a loft room with a ladder need Building Regulations approval?"
    answer: "Structural work does, regardless of what the space is called. Altering how a roof is supported — removing struts, adding a frame, strengthening a floor — is notifiable work and gets inspected. What a ladder-access loft room does not do is meet the requirements for a habitable room, so it cannot be counted as a bedroom when the house is valued or sold. If you want it to count, you need the staircase and everything that comes with it."
  - question: "Can you add a staircase to a loft room later?"
    answer: "Sometimes, and it is worth asking the question before the room is finished rather than after. The constraint is almost never the loft — it is finding somewhere on the floor below for the stairs to land, off an existing landing, with the headroom the regulations require. If there is a realistic position for a stair, it is worth at least knowing where it would go, because the structural work and the floor can be set out with that in mind."
  - question: "Does a loft hatch opening need a balustrade?"
    answer: "A permanent opening in a floor that people walk around needs guarding, and on this job the opening got a proper timber balustrade with newel posts and spindles rather than a bit of handrail. It is the same principle as a stairwell: the floor is now a room people use, and an unguarded hole in it is the obvious hazard. It also finishes the space properly, which matters when the loft is the full width of the house and the opening sits in the middle of it."

Most lofts are not unusable because they are too short. They are unusable because of what is standing in them.

In this one it was the purlin struts — angled timbers running down from the purlins into the middle of the floor, exactly where you would want to walk. The roof needed them. The room could not have them. So the job was finding another way to hold the purlins up.

## What the struts were doing

The purlins are the long horizontal timbers running along the roof slope, carrying the rafters between the gable walls. They are doing real work, and because they span a long way they need propping somewhere along their length.

That propping is the strut: a diagonal timber from the purlin down to a load-bearing wall or a binder below. Structurally sound, and in practical terms it puts a timber brace across the middle of your loft at about head height, usually in pairs.

You can board around them. People do. What you get is a floor with two diagonal timbers through the middle of it and no sensible way to lay the room out.

## The A-frame that replaced them

Rather than work around the struts, the purlins were given a different way of being carried.

A new A-frame was built and fixed so that it picks up the purlin load, and the original struts came out. You can see it in the photograph above — the pale new timber forming the triangle, with the dark original purlins behind it. The frame is doing what the struts were doing, but from a position that leaves the floor clear.

That is the whole trick of it. The load still has to go somewhere, and it still ends up in the structure below. What changes is the route it takes, and therefore how much of the room it spends standing in.

It is worth being plain about one thing: **this is structural work.** Altering how a roof carries itself is designed and calculated properly and it is inspected by Building Control. It is not a job to take a saw to because the strut is in your way.

Two Velux windows went into the slope, which is what turns a boarded loft into somewhere you would actually spend time.

The room was then plastered, the floor laid, softwood skirting fitted, and heating and power run in — there is a radiator under the windows and sockets round the room. None of that is remarkable in itself. What it does is settle the difference between a loft you put boxes in and a loft you use.

The original purlins were left exposed rather than boxed in. On a roof of this age that is the better call: boxing them in would have cost head height and hidden the most interesting thing in the room.

Two more things worth pointing at.

**The cupboard** uses the low space at the eaves, behind louvred double doors in a softwood frame. Louvres rather than solid panels because a cupboard in a roof space benefits from air moving through it. That is dead floor area made useful, which is the same argument as building into the eaves on any loft.

**The opening** has a proper timber balustrade round it — newel posts, spindles, the lot — rather than a bit of handrail screwed to the floor. The loft is a room people walk around in now, and a permanent hole in the middle of the floor needs guarding like any stairwell.

## Why this is a loft room, not a loft conversion

Worth being straight about, because the distinction matters and plenty of people selling this work are not straight about it.

Access here is a **loft ladder**, not a staircase. That one fact is the difference.

A loft conversion creates a **habitable room** under the Building Regulations, and that brings a fixed staircase meeting the pitch and headroom rules, a protected escape route down to the front door, fire doors to the rooms off it, and a completion certificate at the end. [Our Building Regulations guide](/guides/loft-conversion-building-regulations) sets out what that involves.

This is a loft room. Plastered, heated, lit, floored, with the structure altered properly and signed off — a genuinely good space, and the family who owns it uses it. But it is reached by a ladder, so it is not a habitable room in the regulations sense, and it should not be counted as a bedroom when the house is valued or sold.

If what you want is a bedroom that counts, you want a [full loft conversion](/services/extensions-conversions), and the thing that decides whether you can have one is usually not the loft at all — it is whether there is anywhere sensible on the floor below for a staircase to land.
