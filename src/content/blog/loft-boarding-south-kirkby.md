The floor ends up higher than the old ceiling joists, which is the trade-off — you need the headroom for it."
  - question: "Can loft boarding be raised above the insulation?"

## Creating a loft storage floor

Here is the bit that gets missed. The timbers you can see in an ordinary loft are **ceiling joists**, not floor joists. They were sized to hold up a plasterboard ceiling and not much else. They are not there to carry you, a few hundred kilos of boxes and a chest freezer full of last Christmas.

So the floor gets built, not just covered. New 4x2 C16 structural timber was run across the existing ceiling joists, picking up bearing at the walls, to form a proper floor deck above the old ones.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/loft-boarding-south-kirkby/4x2-c16-joists-over-existing-ceiling-joists.webp" alt="New 4x2 C16 timber joists running across the existing ceiling joists to form a loft floor" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>New 4x2 C16 running across the existing ceiling joists — a floor in its own right.</figcaption>
</figure>

Running the new timber **across** the old rather than alongside it does two things at once. It spreads the load over a lot of joists instead of concentrating it on a few, and it lifts the floor level clear of what is already down there. That second point matters more than people expect, and it is what the next section is about.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/loft-boarding-south-kirkby/loft-floor-joists-installed-south-kirkby.webp" alt="New loft floor joists installed across a South Kirkby loft ready for boarding" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>The new floor joists in and levelled across the full span.</figcaption>
</figure>

Noggins went in between the new joists. They stop the timber twisting as it dries out and they stiffen the whole deck, so the finished floor feels solid underfoot instead of springy. It is a small amount of extra work that you feel every time you walk on it.

## Loft boarding over the existing insulation

This is where a lot of loft boarding goes wrong, and it is worth understanding before anyone starts.

Insulation works because it is full of trapped air. Squash it and you throw a good part of that away. Lay boards straight down onto insulation that is already at the right depth and you have just flattened it, and paid for the privilege — you get a floor and you lose insulation you already own.

Raising the floor on new timber solves it. The new 4x2 sits above the old ceiling joists, and the gap that creates is where the insulation lives, at full depth, with the boards never touching it.

<figure>

</figure>

Because the new timber had created that depth, more insulation went in while the floor was open. That is the sensible time to do it — once the chipboard is down and screwed, getting back under it is a job in itself.

## 18mm chipboard loft flooring

The floor was decked in 18mm chipboard flooring, laid across the new joists and screwed down.

Chipboard flooring is the right material for a storage loft. It is stable, it comes in big sheets so there are fewer joints, and at 18mm over joists at sensible centres it gives you a floor you can load up and walk about on without it flexing.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/loft-boarding-south-kirkby/18mm-chipboard-loft-flooring-being-laid.webp" alt="18mm chipboard loft flooring being laid over new timber joists in South Kirkby" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" />

Boards get laid with the joints landing on timber, staggered rather than lining up in a row, and screwed rather than nailed so nothing works loose and starts creaking later. No shortcut workmanship — a loft floor that squeaks every time you cross it is a floor that was rushed.

Setting a ladder out is not just screwing it to the hatch. It has to land in the right place on the landing floor, at a sensible angle, clear of the walls and of anything on them, and it has to sit square so it folds away without fighting you. Get that wrong and you have a ladder nobody uses.

Finally the hatch was trimmed with architrave, so from downstairs it reads as a finished part of the house rather than a hole in the ceiling.

## Loft boarding is not a loft conversion

Worth being straight about this, because the two get mixed up and they are very different jobs.

What was built here is a **storage floor**. It is strong, it is properly insulated underneath, and it will take a serious amount of gear. It is not a room. A [loft conversion](/services/extensions-conversions) is a different piece of work altogether — structure, stairs, headroom, escape, insulation and fire all have to be dealt with, and it goes through building control.

Most people asking about loft boarding do not want a conversion. They want the boxes, the tools, the camping gear and the Christmas decorations out of the spare room and somewhere sound to put them. That is exactly what this is, and it is a fraction of the work.

The floor structure itself is ordinary [first fix carpentry](/services/first-fix-carpentry) — setting out, joists, noggins, decking — the same thinking that goes into any [timber floor](/services/flooring), just done in an awkward space with a roof over your head.
