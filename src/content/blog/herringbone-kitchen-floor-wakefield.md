This was a kitchen floor in Wakefield, fitted in oak herringbone laminate while the kitchen itself was still being put in.

Most of the work on a floor like this happens before a single board is clicked together. The room had a solid floor that needed levelling, it needed a damp proof membrane, and it needed fibreboard over the top of that. Get any of those three wrong and the floor above them fails, however carefully the pattern is cut.

There was also a decision to make first, and it is the one people tend not to think about until the boards arrive.

## Which way should the herringbone run?

Herringbone is not like plank flooring. A plank floor has an obvious direction — down the length of the room, nearly always. Herringbone has two, because the pattern itself is made of blocks running at ninety degrees to each other, and the whole thing can be set out pointing either way.

</figure>

So before anything went down we laid some boards out loose on the floor, in the room, in the actual light, and had a proper look at it from the doorway. The customer made the call with us standing there rather than from a photograph, which is the right way round.

It matters more than it sounds. In a narrow galley, running the pattern along the length draws the eye down the room and makes it feel longer. Running it across shortens it. And because herringbone blocks face two directions, the light falls on them differently depending on the run — the same floor genuinely looks different.

Once that was settled, everything else followed from it, because the starting line and the set-out all depend on the direction.

## Levelling the floor first

The floor needed levelling before anything went on it, and that was done with a self-levelling compound.

A solid floor is rarely as flat as it looks. Old adhesive, patched repairs, a dip where something used to stand — none of it is obvious to the eye and all of it comes through a laminate floor later. Herringbone is less forgiving than plank flooring here, because every block meets three others. A hump that would barely show under long planks puts a rock into a herringbone joint and works it loose over time.

Levelling compound is poured on, finds its own level and sets flat. Then it has to be properly dry before anything covers it. That waiting is part of the job, not a delay in it.

## Damp proof membrane over a solid floor

A solid ground floor is never dry in the way timber is. Moisture moves up through concrete and screed, slowly and continuously, and a floor laid straight onto it will take that moisture into its edges and its core. The boards swell, the joints lift, and it fails from underneath where nobody can see it coming.

So the damp proof membrane went down over the levelled floor, run into the corners and turned up at the perimeter so the floor sits inside a sealed tray rather than on top of a damp one. The laps are taped. It is a cheap material and a quick stage, and it is the one that decides whether the floor is still sound in ten years.

This is the part that gets skipped. You cannot see it once the floor is on, nobody asks about it, and the floor looks exactly the same on the day either way. It just does not last.

## Fibreboard underlay

Fibreboard went down over the membrane.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/herringbone-kitchen-floor-wakefield/fibreboard-underlay-laid-over-dpm-kitchen-floor-wakefield.webp" alt="Green fibreboard underlay boards laid across a kitchen floor over the damp proof membrane, joints staggered" width="1080" height="1920" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Fibreboard across the whole floor, joints staggered, running through into the doorway.</figcaption>
</figure>

It does three things in a kitchen. It takes up the small irregularities that survive levelling, so the floor beds down evenly. It takes the hollow sound out from underfoot, which matters in a hard room with units and tiling in it. And it gives the laminate a consistent, slightly forgiving base to sit on rather than bare cement.

The boards are laid with the joints staggered rather than lining up, so there is no continuous line running across the floor for the laminate above to find.

## Laying the floor

With the base right, the laying is the straightforward part — which is how it should be.

The pattern was set out from the line settled that morning, and worked out from there. The units were already in, so the floor was cut neatly to the plinth line rather than running underneath them, with the expansion gap kept at the perimeter where it is hidden rather than closed up. A floor with nowhere to move will lift, and a kitchen is the last room you want to be taking back up.

The thing worth looking at in that last photograph is the joints. Herringbone shows up a poor base faster than any other floor, because every block is meeting three others and anything out of flat opens a joint. Tight joints and a pattern that runs true across the room are not a laying achievement — they are what you get when the levelling, the membrane and the underlay were all done properly first.

A light oak herringbone also does a lot for a narrow galley kitchen. It gives a plain room some pattern to look at without needing a strong colour, and it reads as a considerably more expensive floor than it is.
