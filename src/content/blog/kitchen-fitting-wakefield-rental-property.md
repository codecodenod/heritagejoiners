This kitchen went into a three-bed terraced house in Wakefield that the owner lets out. The brief was straightforward and it is one we hear a lot from landlords: the old kitchen was tired and dated, the budget was not open-ended, and the new one had to look modern enough that the house showed well to a tenant.

That is a different job from a kitchen somebody is fitting for themselves. It is not about the most timber or the best worktop you can buy. It is about spending the money where it shows and where it lasts, and not spending it anywhere else.

## What the landlord actually needed

Three things, in this order. It had to look current, because a dated kitchen is the thing that loses a viewing. It had to stand up to tenants, which means nothing precious and nothing fiddly. And it had to keep the appliances flexible — space for a freestanding cooker and space for a washing machine, so whoever moves in brings their own and the room still works when they take them away again.

Everything else followed from that.

## Working to a galley kitchen that was already there

</figure>

A galley in a terrace is a narrow room with two runs facing each other and no spare width anywhere. Everything is on show from the doorway, and anything out of line down one run is obvious against the other.

The tiled splashback was sound, so it stayed. That is a real saving on a job like this — no stripping, no retiling, no replastering, no waiting for anything to dry. But it changes how you work. The units and the worktop have to be set out to the tiling that is already on the wall, at the height the tiling already sits at, rather than the wall being made to suit the kitchen. Get the levels out by a few millimetres and you end up with a gap under the tile or a worktop running into it.

That is the usual shape of joinery in an older house. The room gives you what it gives you, and the skill is in working to it rather than wishing it were square.

## Light grey units with the handles built in

The doors and drawer fronts are light grey slab, with the grip routed into the top edge of each one rather than a handle screwed on the face.

There are three good reasons for that in a rental, and they are all practical rather than decorative.

Nothing projects into the walkway, which matters when two people are trying to get past each other in a room this width. There is no handle to work loose, and a loose handle is one of the most common small repairs a landlord gets called about. And it reads as a far more modern kitchen than it otherwise would, for no extra money — a plain slab door with a built-in grip is what people picture when they picture a new kitchen.

The colour does the same job. Light grey keeps a narrow room feeling open, and it is neutral enough that it does not date in the way a strong colour does.

## A white marble effect worktop

The worktop is white with a marble effect running through it, and it is doing more work than it looks.

In a room with grey units and a dark tile border already on the wall, a white top lifts the whole thing and stops it reading as flat. The marble figuring hides the day-to-day marks that a plain white surface shows up straight away, which on a rental is a genuinely useful property. And it wipes down, which is the first question to ask about any surface going into a house you do not live in.

The parts that take the time are the ends and the joints. Worktop ends beside an appliance gap are on permanent show, so they get finished properly, and every joint and scribe gets sealed. Water getting into a worktop joint is how a kitchen starts failing, and it always starts somewhere nobody looks.

## Leaving space for a freestanding cooker and a washing machine

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/kitchen-fitting-wakefield/kitchen-sink-run-washing-machine-space-wakefield.webp" alt="Kitchen worktop run with inset sink under the window and an appliance space left for a washing machine" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>The sink run under the window, with the washing machine space left open underneath the worktop.</figcaption>
</figure>

Both appliance spaces were left open deliberately. Integrated appliances look tidier, but on a let they tie the landlord into supplying and maintaining the machine, and they tie the next tenant into whatever is already there.

Leaving a gap sounds like the easy option. It is not, quite. The opening has to be set out with proper clearance rather than to the nominal width of the appliance, because once the units either side are fixed and the worktop is scribed to the wall, a 600mm gap is no longer 600mm. The worktop ends and the unit sides that frame the opening are visible from the doorway, so they get the same attention as anything else. And the services behind the gap — water and waste for the washing machine, the cooker point — have to come out in a position where a machine can actually push back into the space.

The cooker space sits under the existing extractor, so it was set out to line up with the hood that was staying rather than simply centred on the run.

The sink is an inset stainless steel sink and drainer under the window, which is where it was and where it still makes the most sense in a room this shape.

## Keeping the cost down without it looking cheap

This is the part landlords ask about most, so it is worth setting out what actually saved money here and what did not.

What saved money: keeping the sound tiled splashback instead of stripping and retiling. Keeping the existing layout, so no plumbing or electrics had to be moved. Standard cabinet sizes rather than anything made to measure. Freestanding appliance spaces rather than integrated units.

What did not get cut: the setting out, the levels, the scribes to the wall, the sealing. Those cost time rather than materials, and they are exactly what separates a kitchen that still looks right in five years from one that starts dropping doors and lifting joints in two.

That is the trade-off worth understanding. A cost-effective kitchen is a decision about specification, not a decision about workmanship. Cheap units fitted well will outlast good units fitted badly, every time — and on a rental, the thing that gets you called back is never the door style.
