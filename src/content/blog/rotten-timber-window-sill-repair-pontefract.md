faqs:
  - question: "Can a rotten window sill be repaired instead of replaced?"
    answer: "Usually, yes. A window sill rots from the top and the front edge first, where water sits, and it often does that long before the rest of the window is in any trouble. If the decay is local and there is firm timber to work back to, the failed section can be cut out and new timber spliced in. You only need a new sill — or a new window — when the rot has gone right through and there is nothing sound left to join onto."
  - question: "How do you know if a window sill is rotten?"
    answer: "Press it. Sound timber is hard and gives nothing; decayed timber goes soft and a screwdriver or a bradawl will sink into it. Look for a finish that has flaked or lifted, dark patches that stay damp after everything else has dried, open joints at the corners, and filler that has cracked away from the timber around it. Soft timber under an intact-looking coating is very common — the coating holds the shape long after the wood underneath has gone."
  - question: "Is filler good enough for a rotten window sill?"
    answer: "For shallow, local defects in timber that is still sound underneath, a two-part exterior filler is the right answer. What filler cannot do is carry anything. If a corner has gone soft all the way through, filler has nothing to hold on to and it will push out in a season or two. The test is what is underneath: firm timber, fill it; nothing firm, cut it out and splice in new timber."
  - question: "What timber is used to repair a window sill?"
    answer: "On this job the splices were cut from sapele, a durable hardwood that machines cleanly, takes an exterior finish well and stands up to weather on an exposed sill. The important thing is that the new timber is at least as durable as what it is going into, and that the grain runs the same way, so old and new move together instead of opening up at the joint."
  - question: "What causes timber window sills to rot?"
    answer: "Water sitting where it cannot dry. A sill is the one part of a window that water runs onto and collects on, so it takes the most punishment. Once the exterior finish thins or breaks, water gets into the timber, and if the perimeter sealant has also failed it gets in behind the frame as well. Add a few years with no maintenance and the sill and the bottoms of the stiles are always the first places to go."
  - question: "How long will a spliced sill repair last?"
    answer: "As long as the rest of the window, if it is done properly and then maintained. A splice cut back to firm timber, bonded, and finished as part of the window is not a temporary patch — it becomes part of the sill. What shortens its life is the same thing that caused the original problem: leaving the coating and the sealant to fail and letting water get back in."

Timber window sills are almost always the first part of a window to go. Water runs onto them, sits on them and works into any gap it can find, and once the finish has thinned they take the punishment for the whole window.

This one was on a commercial office building on Micklegate in Pontefract. Eleven timber windows, and nothing done to any of them since the owners took the building on in 2008. Here is how far the rot had actually gone, why filler on its own would have been a waste of everybody's time, and how the sills were saved with spliced-in timber instead of new windows.

## Where timber window sills rot first

Look at any weathered timber window and the damage will be in the same places: the top face of the sill, the front edge, and the bottom few inches of the stiles where they land on it. That is where water collects and where it stays longest.

The rest of a window can be in reasonable condition while the sill is finished. That is worth knowing, because it is the difference between a local repair and a quote for replacement windows.

On these windows the coating had worn thin or failed completely on the exposed elevations. The timber underneath was dry and grey. The sealant between the frames and the brickwork had cracked and pulled away, so water was getting in behind the frame as well as sitting on top of the sill.

## Finding out how far the rot has gone

You cannot price or plan a sill repair by looking at it. Decayed timber often sits behind a coating that still looks more or less intact, so the first job is to find the edge of the sound wood.

That means scraping the loose material off and probing the timber until it stops being soft. On a bad corner the decay usually runs further than it looks from outside — under the stile, back along the sill, into the joint.

We set out a written repair schedule for every numbered window on the building before any of it was priced, so what was straightforward maintenance and what was a genuine repair were separated from the start rather than discovered halfway through.

## Filler, or a splice repair?

This is the decision that matters, and it is not a matter of taste. It depends entirely on what is left underneath.

Where the defect was shallow and there was firm timber below it, a two-part exterior filler was the right answer. It was filled, shaped and sanded to follow the existing profile so the sill still read as one piece.

Where a corner had gone soft all the way through, filler was no use. Filler does not carry anything — it needs sound timber to key into. Put it into decayed wood and you have hidden the problem rather than repaired it, and it will push back out within a season or two.

On two of the front windows the sills and the bottoms of the stiles were past filling. Those got new timber.

## Cutting a sapele splice into a window sill

A splice repair means cutting the failed section out square, back to timber that is firm, and letting a new piece in to replace exactly what was lost. Done properly it is not a patch stuck on the outside — it becomes part of the sill.

The new pieces were cut from sapele. It is durable, it machines cleanly and it takes an exterior finish well, which is what you want on a sill that is going to sit out in Yorkshire weather. Each piece was shaped to follow the existing sill and stile profiles rather than being left as a flat block.

That corner is the detail worth looking at. Two splices meeting where the stile lands on the sill, cut in flush and running through the existing profile. Once it is coated you will not pick it out from the original timber, which is the whole point.

## Sealing the repair so the water stays out

Repairing the timber and stopping there is half a job. If the perimeter sealant has failed — and on a window with a rotten sill it usually has — water will simply carry on getting in behind the frame and you will be back at the same corner in a few years.

## Repair or replace a rotten window sill?

People are told far more often than they should be that a rotten sill means a new window. Sometimes it genuinely does. Most of the time it does not.

The questions are simple enough: how much sound timber is left, where is the water getting in, and can the failed section be joined onto something firm. If the answers are "most of it", "here", and "yes", it is a repair — and a repair keeps the original window, costs less disruption and holds its appearance in a building where new units would look wrong.
