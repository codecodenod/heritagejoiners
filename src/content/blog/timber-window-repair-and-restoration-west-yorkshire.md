Most people who ring up about a timber window have already been told it needs replacing. Often they have been told it by somebody who sells windows.

Sometimes that is the right answer. Far more often it is not, because timber windows do not fail evenly. They fail in specific places, for specific reasons, and the rest of the window carries on being perfectly sound timber. A casement rots along its bottom rail while the stiles and the glazing above it are untouched. A sill goes soft at one corner while the rest of the frame is firm. Replace the whole unit and you have thrown away a great deal of sound timber to deal with a defect measured in inches.

This is a long article because timber window repair is a long subject. It covers how these windows actually fail, how you work out whether what is in front of you can be repaired, how splice and let-in repairs are cut, what is involved in getting sashes and casements working again, and how listed buildings and conservation areas change the picture. The examples are two real jobs — eleven windows on a commercial building in Pontefract, and one casement in a Grade II listed cottage in Bradford — both written up in full elsewhere on this site and linked where they are relevant.

## How timber windows actually fail

Decay in a timber window is almost never a mystery. It is water that has got into the wood and has not been able to dry out again, and there are only so many places it gets in.

### The coating goes first, not the timber

An exterior coating is the window's only real protection, and it does not fail all at once. It thins on the faces that take the sun and the weather, it splits at a corner or along a joint, it lifts around a nail hole or a crack. Any one of those is a way in.

Once water is behind the coating it has nowhere to go. The film that was keeping it out is now keeping it in. The timber underneath stays damp, and permanently damp timber is exactly the condition wet rot needs. This is why a window can look tired but basically intact from the ground and be soft when you get a screwdriver into it — the coating holds the shape long after the wood underneath has gone.

### Sills and bottom rails

The bottom of a window takes the worst of it, every time, on every building.

A sill is the one part that water runs onto and then sits on. Rain comes down the glass, collects along the bottom, and works into any open joint it can find. On a casement the same thing happens to the bottom rail, the horizontal timber along the bottom of the opening part, running between the two upright stiles. It carries the bottom edge of the glass and it spends its life with water standing on it.

Then there are the corners. Where the stile lands on the sill there is a joint, and a joint is a gap, and a gap holds water. On nearly every rotten window job the two worst places are the middle of the sill and the corners where the uprights meet it.

### Glazing rebates and beads

The rebate is the step the glass sits in, and the bead holds it there. Both are bedded, and bedding does not last forever.

When bedding around a glazing bead fails, water runs down the glass and goes straight in behind it rather than off the face of the window. You can put two good coats on the outside of a window and achieve nothing at all if the water is getting in behind the beads. It is the same principle as a leaking roof — coating the surface does not help if the water is going underneath it.

### Joints, frames and opening sections

Frame joints open up as timber moves, and once open they take water. Opening casements and sashes have a further problem: they are handled, they are knocked, they swell and shrink, and the gap they close against changes shape over the years.

A casement that has dropped on its hinges will be binding at one corner and standing proud at the other. The gap that was keeping weather out is no longer closed, and the parts of it taking the rub are losing their coating faster than everything else.

### Sashes that stick, rattle or will not move

A sliding sash that will not open is usually one of a small number of things: a coating that has bridged the gap and effectively glued it shut, debris packed into the run, binding where the timber has swelled, failed seals, or a mechanical problem with whatever is carrying the weight.

That last one matters, because it decides who fixes it. A traditional box sash runs on cords over pulleys with counterweights hidden in the boxes either side, and when a cord parts the sash stops balancing. A later window may run on spring balances instead, which is a different mechanism with different parts. Either way, a sash that will not move is not always a joinery problem, and it is worth knowing which it is before anybody starts taking things apart.

## Working out whether a window can be repaired

This is the part of the job that decides everything after it, and it is done at the window.

### Press it

Sound timber is hard and gives nothing. Decayed timber goes soft, and a screwdriver or a bradawl sinks into it. That is the whole test, and it is worth doing systematically rather than at the one place that looks bad — along the sill, into the corners, up the bottom of each stile, along the bottom rail, around the beads.

What you are building up is a map of where the sound timber stops. Look as well for a coating that has flaked or lifted, dark patches that stay damp after everything else has dried, joints that have opened at the corners, and old filler that has cracked away from the wood around it. Filler separating from timber is a reliable sign that somebody has already tried to deal with this once, and that there was nothing solid to hold it.

### How far has it gone?

The question is not whether there is rot. There is almost always rot. The question is how much of the window is still sound, and that is what separates a repair from a replacement.

When the decay is local — a sill corner, a bottom rail, the foot of a stile — and there is firm timber either side of it, a repair is straightforward joinery. When decay has gone through several of the main structural members, when the frame or sash is badly out of square, or when the joints have failed right across the unit, there comes a point where there is not enough sound timber left to carry a reliable repair. That is a genuine replacement, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than selling somebody a repair that will not hold.

### What filler can and cannot do

A two-part exterior filler is a proper material used on plenty of jobs, and on the Pontefract job most of the local defects were dealt with exactly that way — failed material cut out, the timber underneath treated, the defect filled, then shaped and sanded to follow the existing profile.

What filler cannot do is carry anything. It needs sound timber to key into. Put it into decayed wood and it has nothing to hold on to, and it will push back out within a season or two, usually lifting the coating with it.

So the test is simply what is underneath. Firm timber, and the defect is shallow and local: fill it. Nothing firm to key into, or the section has gone soft all the way through: cut it out and let new timber in. Deciding that correctly is most of the skill, and getting it wrong in either direction costs the client money — either a repair that fails, or a window replaced that did not need to be.

## Splice and let-in repairs

This is the core of timber window repair, and it is ordinary joinery done carefully rather than anything exotic. The principle is the same whether you are dealing with a sill, a bottom rail or the foot of a stile: cut out what has failed, back to timber that is firm, and let a new piece in to replace exactly what was lost.

Done properly it is not a patch stuck on the outside. It becomes part of the window.

### Cutting back to firm timber

The first job is finding where the sound wood starts. Decayed timber has no strength and will not hold anything, so there is no point stopping while it is still soft — you cut back until a chisel is biting into firm timber, and not before.

  <figcaption>The rot chiselled out of the Bradford casement rail. The pile of chippings answers the question of whether it was really rotten.</figcaption>
</figure>

The quantity of material that comes out tells you what you were dealing with, and it is usually more than the client expected from the outside.

The other half of cutting back is knowing when to stop. You are not trying to remove as much as possible — you are trying to remove everything that has failed and nothing that has not. On a historic window every piece of original timber you keep is worth keeping, so the cut is taken to sound wood and no further. Cut the opening square and clean, because a square opening is one you can fit a new piece into accurately.

### Choosing the replacement timber

The new piece has to be at least as durable as what it is going into. Putting something less durable into an exposed sill means you will be back at the same corner before long, and you will have disturbed sound timber to achieve it.

On the Micklegate job the splices were cut from sapele. It is durable, it machines cleanly and it takes an exterior finish well, which is what you want on a sill that is going to sit out in Yorkshire weather. Softwood repairs are perfectly sound in the right place and Historic England are clear that a well-cut repair works even on external softwood frames, but on the most exposed element of the window a durable hardwood earns its place.

Grain direction is the detail that gets overlooked. The new timber has to run the same way as the timber it is joining, so that old and new move together with the weather instead of opening up at the joint the first time it turns.

### Matching the profile

A window section is not a plain rectangle. There is the bottom edge, the rebate the glass sits in, the angle that sheds water, whatever moulding the original joiner put on it. New timber has to follow all of it.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/timber-window-sill-repair/sapele-splice-repair-window-sill-dry-fitted.webp" alt="New sapele pieces shaped and dry-fitted along the bottom of a timber window sill before bonding" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>New sapele shaped to the existing profile and offered up along the sill before any adhesive goes near it.</figcaption>
</figure>

Get the profile wrong and the repair reads as a patch from ten feet away, which on a period building defeats the point of repairing it at all. Each piece is shaped to follow the existing sill and stile profiles rather than being left as a flat block, and it is worked to the original rather than the original being worked to suit the new piece.

### Cutting the joint

A let-in repair is a joint between two pieces of timber, with proper bearing, doing the same job the original did. Where the geometry suits it, a long angled joint — a scarf — gives more glue area and more bearing than a square butt, and keeps the join from falling on a single line across the section. On a smaller let-in repair into a rail, a clean square housing cut back to firm timber is the appropriate joint. The right answer depends on what you are repairing and how much sound material you have to work with.

  <img src="/images/blog/listed-casement-window-repair-bradford/new-timber-let-into-casement-window-bottom-rail.webp" alt="New timber let into the bottom rail of a casement window beside the retained original painted timber" width="1920" height="1080" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>New timber let into the Bradford rail against the retained original. The joint is cut, not filled.</figcaption>
</figure>

Everything gets dry-fitted first. You want the joints tight and the profile running through before any adhesive is involved, because once it is bonded you are committed.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/timber-window-sill-repair/sapele-splice-let-into-window-sill-and-stile.webp" alt="Sapele splice let into the bottom of a window stile and along the sill, cut in flush with the existing timber" width="1080" height="1920" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Let in flush at the corner — one splice into the bottom of the stile, another running along the sill.</figcaption>
</figure>

That corner is the detail worth looking at. Two splices meeting where the stile lands on the sill, cut in flush and running through the existing profile. Once it is coated you will not pick it out from the original timber, which is the whole point of doing it this way.

### Keeping the glass in

Where the decay is below the glazing line there is no reason to take the glass out, and good reason not to. On the Bradford casement the leaded glazing stayed exactly where it was throughout the repair. Original or early glass has a texture and a slight unevenness that new glass does not, it is very easily broken once it is out, and it is not easily matched. Leaving it in is both cheaper and better for the building.

### Protecting the joint and the end grain

End grain takes up water far faster than a face does, so any exposed end grain left by a repair gets treated and sealed before the finish goes on. Bare and repaired timber is treated before filling and coating — the part nobody sees, and the part that decides how long the job lasts.

## Sashes, casements and the moving parts

A window that has been repaired but still does not open properly is only half restored.

### Sliding sashes

A traditional box sash hangs on cords that run over pulleys to counterweights concealed in the boxes at each side. When a cord parts, the sash drops and stops balancing; the repair is to renew the cord, and it is worth doing both sides rather than waiting for the second one. Pulleys that are stiff or worn, and weights that have become detached, are part of the same job. This is the arrangement on most genuinely old sash windows and it is worth preserving where it exists, because it is repairable almost indefinitely.

Later windows often use spring balances instead, which is a different mechanism entirely. On the Micklegate job six of the upper sliding sashes were reported as not opening. Each accessible sash was checked for binding, debris, failed seals and mechanical problems, and the front upper sashes were released and put back into working order. The inspection also turned up worn springs and sash balances — mechanical work needing the correct components, so it was kept separate from the exterior maintenance rather than bodged in alongside it. That distinction is worth making on any quote: joinery and mechanical replacement parts are two different jobs.

### Casements, hinges and stays

A casement that binds, drops or will not close against its rebate is usually a hinge or an alignment problem rather than a timber problem, though years of binding will have worn the coating off wherever it has been rubbing.

Hinges and traditional ironmongery are worth assessing properly before anything is replaced. Old ironmongery is frequently better made than what you would buy to replace it, and a stay, a casement fastener or a set of butts that has been painted into immobility for twenty years will often clean up and work. Where something genuinely has failed, matching the pattern matters on a period building for the same reason matching a timber profile does.

### Draughts

Improving how a window closes is largely about getting the moving part to meet the fixed part properly again — correcting the alignment, dealing with the binding, renewing failed seals where they exist. A window that has been repaired, eased and closed up properly against its rebate is a significantly better window to live with than it was, without changing what it looks like.

## Preparation and finishing

A finish is only ever as good as the preparation underneath it, and preparation is where most of the time on a refurbishment actually goes.

Loose coating is scraped off and the timber sanded back to a firm edge. Failed material and debris are cleared out of sill corners, lower rails and the other exposed areas — all the places where it accumulates and holds water. Bare and repaired timber is treated before any filling or coating.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/case-studies/dunster-windows/treating-rotten-timber-wood-preserver-pontefract.webp" alt="Bare timber being treated with wood preserver before filling and coating on a window repair in Pontefract" width="1080" height="1920" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Treating bare and repaired timber before filling and coating. Nobody sees this stage; it decides how long the job lasts.</figcaption>
</figure>

If the surface underneath is soft or dirty, the coating will lift off it inside a season or two. No shortcut workmanship.

### Dealing with the water, not just the timber

Repairing the timber and stopping there is half a job. If the perimeter sealant has failed — and on a window with a rotten sill it usually has — water will carry on getting in behind the frame and you will be back at the same corner in a few years.

On the Pontefract job the old perimeter sealant had failed all round. It was cut out of the accessible joints and renewed after preparation, closing the junction between the timber frame and the surrounding brickwork, and failed bedding around the sides and lower glazing beads was dealt with at the same time. One short section beside a repaired lintel had to wait for adjacent cement work to cure before it could be sealed, and was finished on the last visit rather than sealed over something that was not ready.

### The coating

On Micklegate a mahogany/red-brown exterior woodstain was chosen to stay close to the building's established appearance, and two full coats were applied to the specified system, over everything including the repairs, so the new timber is protected the same as the rest.

A woodstain protects the timber without turning it into an opaque painted surface, so the grain and the natural variation still show. A third coat was considered on that job and we advised against it — it would have darkened the windows and hidden more of the grain for no practical gain. Recommend what the timber needs; do not add work because it can be charged for.

### Maintenance afterwards

What shortens the life of a repair is exactly what caused the original decay: letting the coating and the sealant fail and allowing water back into the timber. A look over the windows once a year, particularly along the sills, the bottom rails and the corners, catches a coating that is thinning long before it becomes a joinery job. That is the whole of window maintenance, and it is considerably cheaper than the alternative.

## Listed buildings and conservation areas

These are two different designations and they are routinely confused. It is worth being precise, because the controls are not the same.

A **listed building** is protected in its own right. The legal test sits in **section 7 of the Planning (Listed Buildings and Conservation Areas) Act 1990**: consent is required for works of alteration that *"would affect its character as a building of special architectural or historic interest"*. A like-for-like repair — same timber, same profile, putting back what was there — does not normally change that character, so it is generally treated as repair rather than alteration. Replacement is treated differently. Bradford Council's guidance is explicit that *"the replacement of a door or window will always require Listed Building Consent, as this would be classed as an alteration rather than repair"*.

A **conservation area** designation protects the character of an area rather than one building. An unlisted building inside a conservation area — like the Micklegate job — is generally less constrained than a listed building, but that does not mean there are no controls at all, and the local authority's own appraisal for that area will usually say what matters about it.

**One caution, and it is a real one.** The statutory test involves judgement, and what affects a building's character varies from one building to the next and one council to the next. Do not take a blog post as clearance for your own job. If there is any doubt about what you are proposing, ask the conservation team at the council before anything starts — they would far rather answer a question early than deal with unauthorised work afterwards.

### Why repair is the preferred approach on historic buildings

Historic England's position on traditional windows is not ambiguous. Complete replacement *"should be a last resort, for example if they are beyond repair"*, and *"careful repair is preferable to new work as it will retain the historic fabric of your home"*.

They also take on the objection people usually raise:

> "There is a widely held view that repairs are short lived and not as good as new replacement windows, but this need not be the case if you use an experienced joiner and the right materials, even for external softwood frames."

That is the crux of it. A repair is only as good as the person cutting it. Done properly, a let-in repair is not a stopgap — it becomes part of the window and lasts as long as the timber around it.

That is also why heritage window repairs are worth treating as a distinct piece of work rather than general maintenance. Matching an existing profile, choosing timber that will sit properly next to what is already there, and knowing how much original fabric has to be kept are judgements that come from having done it before, not from a product catalogue.

## Repair or replacement: how the decision actually gets made

Setting the two against each other honestly, rather than as a sales pitch either way.

**Repair genuinely has the advantage when** the decay is localised and there is firm timber to join onto; when the window's proportions, profiles, glazing bars or glass are part of what the building looks like; when the building is listed, where repair is the preferred approach and replacement is an alteration needing consent; and when a run of windows has to match across an elevation, because one new unit among old ones is usually obvious.

**Replacement is the honest answer when** decay has gone through several of the main structural members with nothing sound to work back to; when the frame or sash is badly distorted; when the joints have failed right across the unit; or when repeated poor-quality repairs have left too little original material to carry another one. At some point another patch is throwing good money after bad, and it is better to say so.

What we will not do is pretend a repair always wins. It does not. It wins more often than people are told it does, which is a different claim. The judgement is worked through in [repair or replace?](/repair-or-replace) and, specifically for windows, in [can timber windows be repaired?](/blog/can-timber-windows-be-repaired).

Rob is a fully qualified apprentice trained bench hand joiner, time served, with over 35 years of workshop and site experience, a good deal of it in old buildings. Timber windows draw on both halves of that: the bench side for cutting a splice that matches an existing profile, and the site side for working out what is actually wrong with a window in a wall rather than on a bench.

Heritage Joiners takes on timber window repairs, sill and bottom rail repairs, splice and let-in repairs, sash releasing and timber window restoration across Pontefract, Wakefield, Castleford, Bradford, Leeds and the wider West Yorkshire area — including heritage window repairs on listed and period properties, where keeping the original joinery is the point of the exercise. The work sits alongside our wider [sash and timber window repair](/services/windows) and [timber repair and restoration](/services/timber-repair) services.

If somebody has told you your windows need replacing, it is worth getting a second opinion before you agree to anything. [Send Rob some photos](/contact) — the whole window, and a close-up of the damaged area — and he will give you an honest view on whether you are looking at a rail, a sill or a window.
